\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All numbers in this section are generated from the stored result files (\texttt{CLAIMS.md} lists each one). Intervals are 95\%
unless stated, from resampling the \nTestSigners{} test signers. Seeds are averaged first. ``Examples per sign'' $k$ means
references for the nearest-neighbour methods and training examples for the learned models; ``all'' uses every training video of
the sign.

\subsection{Pre-registered hypotheses}
\textbf{H1 is not supported.} We expected DTW to beat each learned model at vocabulary 50 with $k\le5$. Instead every learned
model is above DTW at $k=1$, 2 and 5: \nHOneBelowZero{} of the nine paired differences have 97.5\% intervals wholly on the learned model's side of zero (\texttt{results/tables/hypotheses.csv}). For example, at $k=1$ the CNN is \difCnnVFiftyKOne{} points above DTW
(95\% interval \difLoCnnVFiftyKOne{} to \difHiCnnVFiftyKOne{}), and at $k=5$ it is \difCnnVFiftyKFive{} points above
(\difLoCnnVFiftyKFive{} to \difHiCnnVFiftyKFive{}). \textbf{H2 is supported}: with all training videos each learned model is
above DTW by about \difCnnVFiftyKAll{} (CNN), \difTransVFiftyKAll{} (Transformer) and \difGruVFiftyKAll{} (BiGRU) points, and the intervals remain above zero when adjusted for three comparisons (\hTwoAdjSupported).

\subsection{No crossover in the tested range}
We looked for the number of examples per sign at which learned models overtake DTW and found none: at both vocabularies the best
learned model is at or above DTW from $k=1$ upwards (smallest such $k$: \crossoverTen{} at 10 signs, \crossoverFifty{} at 50).
In \nPairedLearnedExcl{} of the \nPairedLearned{} comparisons of a learned model with DTW (3 models, 6 values of $k$, 2 vocabularies) the 95\% interval excludes
zero, and in \nPairedLearnedAhead{} the learned model's point estimate is higher (exploratory; no multiplicity correction). The gap is smallest at $k=1$, where every method is weak: at 50 signs no method
exceeds \accTransVFiftyKOne\% (Transformer, the best) with one example per sign. Euclidean 1-NN is not worse than DTW at 50 signs:
it is ahead by \difEuclidVFiftyKTwo, \difEuclidVFiftyKFive{} and \difEuclidVFiftyKTen{} points at $k=2$, 5 and 10 (intervals exclude zero) and indistinguishable at $k=1$, 20 and ``all'' (\difEuclidVFiftyKOne, \difEuclidVFiftyKTwenty{} and \difEuclidVFiftyKAll{} points). At 10 signs DTW is clearly ahead
of Euclidean 1-NN (interval excluding zero) only with all training videos (Euclidean minus DTW: \difEuclidVTenKAll{} points, interval
\difLoEuclidVTenKAll{} to \difHiEuclidVTenKAll{}), while Euclidean 1-NN is ahead at $k=2$ (\difEuclidVTenKTwo{} points).

\subsection{DTW variants}
DBA prototypes (one barycentre per sign) are poor and get worse with more references per sign at 50 signs
(\accDbaVFiftyKOne\% at $k=1$, \accDbaVFiftyKAll\% with all videos), so we do not recommend averaging signer variants into a single
template. On one fixed draw (50 signs, 10 examples per sign, seed 0), exact DTW scores \accDtwSeedZeroVFiftyKTen\%, FastDTW
(radius 1) \accFastDtwVFiftyKTen\% and the original repository's pipeline \accLegacyVFiftyKTen\%; the intervals overlap
completely (\accLoLegacyVFiftyKTen--\accHiLegacyVFiftyKTen\% for the original pipeline), so these variants are not
distinguishable at this sample size. FastDTW is not faster here: our compiled exact DTW takes \latDtwKTen{} ms per query against 500 references on one thread, while the pure-Python FastDTW takes \latFastDtwKTen{} ms, so this comparison mostly reflects the two implementations; we did not compare the algorithms at equal implementation quality.

\subsection{Preprocessing matters more for the template-matching baselines}
On validation signers (50 signs, 10 examples per sign), DTW with the naive default of zero-filled missing hands and raw features
scored \tuneNaiveDtw\%, against \tuneBestDtw\% for the configuration chosen on validation (frames without a hand dropped,
standardised features, band of 8 frames). The learned models were much less sensitive: best validation accuracy with zero-filled
gaps versus the chosen setting was \tuneZeroCnn\% vs \tuneBestCnn\% for the CNN. Every family chose to drop frames without a
detected hand. We give each family its own validation-selected preprocessing so that no method is held back by an arbitrary
default; the naive default would have made the DTW baseline look \tuneGapDtw{} points worse on validation.

Across every setting we ran, the best DTW result was \ablBestDtwKFive\% at $k=5$ and \ablBestDtwKAll\% with all videos, against
\ablBestCnnKFive\% and \ablBestCnnKAll\% for the best CNN setting, so no feature or preprocessing choice we tried closes the gap. These
maxima are over many settings on the test signers and are therefore optimistic for both families.

\subsection{Augmentation, one- and two-handed signs, and per-sign errors}
Light augmentation (time stretch, noise, scale) changed the learned models by at most a few points: for the CNN at 50 signs,
\accCnnAugVFiftyKOne\% versus \accCnnVFiftyKOne\% at $k=1$ and \accCnnAugVFiftyKAll\% versus \accCnnVFiftyKAll\% with all videos. The
learned models' lead over DTW is therefore not an artefact of leaving them un-augmented. We could not compare one- and two-handed
signs: under the pre-registered rule (both hands detected in at least half of the hand-present frames), \nTwoHandedSigns{} of the
50 signs are two-handed and \nOneHandedSigns{} are one-handed, consistent with PopSign's one-handed focus.

With all training videos, DTW is below 50\% on \nSignsDtwBelowHalf{} of the 50 signs, with the lowest being \worstSignDtw{}
(\worstSignDtwAcc\%), whereas the CNN is below 50\% on \nSignsCnnBelowHalf{} signs (lowest: \cnnWorstSign, \cnnWorstSignAcc\%). The most
frequent DTW confusion is \topConfTrue{} recognised as \topConfPred{} (\topConfN{} test queries). PopSign has no phonological
annotation, so we make no claim about why signs are confused; the full lists are in \texttt{results/tables}.

\subsection{Cost: training and inference}
Template matching needs no training but its inference cost grows with the number of references: with 500 references (10 per sign)
one query takes \latDtwKTen{} ms (95th percentile \latPDtwKTen{} ms) on one thread of an Apple M4 Max with our compiled DTW, and
\latDtwKAll{} ms with all \latRefsAll{} training videos as references (Euclidean nearest neighbour: \latEuclidKTen{} and \latEuclidKAll{} ms). A forward
pass of a trained model takes \latCnn{} ms (CNN), \latTrans{} ms (Transformer) and \latGru{} ms (BiGRU) on one CPU thread, independent
of the number of training examples. Training the CNN and Transformer took on average \trainSecCnnKTen{} and \trainSecTransKTen{}
seconds at 10 examples per sign and \trainSecCnnKAll{} and \trainSecTransKAll{} seconds with all videos on the Apple GPU (BiGRU:
\trainSecGruKTen{} and \trainSecGruKAll{} seconds; training times varied with load on the shared machine). Latency was measured with other
processes running, and no low-power device was available. These costs exclude MediaPipe landmark extraction
(\extractHours{} wall-clock hours for the 50 signs on this machine).

\subsection{Coverage}
On PopSign, vocabularies of 100 and 250 signs were not run: landmark extraction for 50 signs took \extractHours{} hours on this machine and
the larger vocabularies would have taken several times longer. The vocabulary sweep up to 300 signs is on ASL Citizen
(Section~\ref{sec:replication}). Soft-DTW prototypes, planned in the pre-registration, were not run. Of the \nVideosTest{} test queries at 50 signs,
\nNoHandTest{} had no detected hand and is counted as an error; the training and validation splits have \nNoHandTrain{} and \nNoHandVal{} such videos.
