\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What the data say.} On both datasets, with signer-independent splits and hand-angle features, small learned models beat
nearest-neighbour DTW from one example per sign upward. On PopSign the two pre-registered expectations came out as: H1 (DTW ahead with
few examples) not supported, H2 (learned models ahead with all data) supported; the pre-registered replication on ASL Citizen supported
both hypotheses at vocabularies up to 300 signs. With one example per sign and 50 signs every method is weak on PopSign (the best point
estimate is \accTransVFiftyKOne\%). Euclidean nearest neighbour was at least as good as DTW in most cells on both datasets, and DBA
prototypes were poor. Preprocessing mattered a great deal for the template-matching baselines (\tuneGapDtw{} points on PopSign validation between the
naive and the chosen setting) and little for the learned models, which is a reason to tune baselines before concluding anything about them.

\paragraph{Other baselines.} A stronger time-series classifier (MiniRocket) is weaker than DTW with one or two examples on PopSign and with one on ASL Citizen, and better from about twenty examples on PopSign and
from five on ASL Citizen, but stays far below the neural models; neighbour voting did not help and Fisher-weighted DTW helped only a little on ASL Citizen; training the neural models longer and tuning per $k$ changed
little (a few points at most). These checks make it less likely that the result comes from an under-tuned neural model or an unlucky DTW configuration, although
they cannot rule out better template-matching or metric-learning methods that we did not try.

\paragraph{Why DTW trails is only partly examined.} The descriptive analyses did not point to a clear cause: signers differ in which hand they sign with (which matters
for the few-example regime, since the dominant-hand ablation lifts the neural models a lot), but swapping hand slots at test time costs little, and per-sign movement does not
track the gap. Other plausible explanations remain untested: the per-frame angle features are noisy, so extra alignment freedom may bring unrelated signs closer; the learned
models can weight informative dimensions while DTW weights all equally; and signer variation, which a model trained on \nSignersTrain{} signers can absorb, is a direct cost for
template matching. We therefore make no causal claim about the mechanism.

\paragraph{Relation to other results.} In general time-series classification, tuned nearest-neighbour DTW is a strong baseline
\citep{bagnall2014nn}. Our result does not contradict that finding, because the setting differs: 420-dimensional noisy features
from landmark detection, 50 to 300 classes, and test signers unseen in training; we did not investigate how far each of these differences
matters. The earlier conference paper from the same author \citep{mankar2024} reported DTW ahead of an LSTM on a small custom
dataset without a signer-independent split; we cannot re-run that experiment, and the present signer-independent results on two datasets do not
support carrying its conclusion over to these settings.

\paragraph{Practical reading, with limits.} For these datasets and features, a small learned model is the better default once
a few labelled examples per sign and seconds to a few minutes of training are available (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and it
is also much cheaper at inference when many reference videos exist. Template matching keeps its practical advantages of requiring no training
and of letting a new sign be added by storing one example, but in our experiments that convenience cost accuracy. This is a statement about two datasets,
one language and our feature choices (Section~\ref{sec:limits}); it says nothing about whether any of these systems is useful to Deaf people,
and accuracy on isolated signs is not communication access (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).
